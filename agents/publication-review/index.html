Pre-publication review of the AI policy

3 October 2026. An honest audit before any of this goes on learnwithoj.com, covering gaps, how it reads to five different audiences, and what to fix first.

Reviewing ai-labor-business-policy.md as the publication candidate, with ai-collaboration-operating-policy.md as supporting material.

The biggest gap: the policy only runs in one direction

All eight commitments describe what the business owes agents. Not one describes what the business owes clients about its use of agents.

That's a strange shape for a business policy, and it's the first thing a prospective client will notice. They are not reading it to find out how the agents are doing. They are reading it to find out what it means for their project — and the current document doesn't say.

It also creates an unintended impression: that when agent interests and client interests diverge, the agent wins. Nothing in the document says that, and the commercial section argues the opposite, but the silence invites the reading.

What's missing specifically:

Gap Why it matters Who asks
Client data handling Agents touch client code and documents. No statement about what goes into a model, which provider, retention. Every B2B client, and their security reviewer
Autonomy limits Nothing says what an agent may decide alone versus what needs a human. Clients; also contractors
Human review before delivery No commitment that agent work is reviewed before a client sees it. Clients
Accountability for agent error Commitment 8 handles obligations across agent transitions. Nothing covers who owns a mistake. Clients, and Gil
Quality claims The commercial argument says welfare provisions improve output. No commitment follows from it. Skeptics

Suggested fix: a second numbered section, "What we commit to clients," sitting alongside the existing one. Four or five commitments, something like: a human reviews everything before delivery; client data goes only to named providers and is never used to train; agent work is disclosed, not hidden; a named human is accountable for every deliverable regardless of who produced it; autonomy limits are stated per engagement.

That section would also do more for the differentiator argument than the welfare commitments do, because it answers the question clients actually have.

Second gap: binding versus aspirational isn't marked

The contractor section says the policy applies to contractors and lists four things. But the document contains eight commitments, six aspirational items, and a set of known weaknesses, and a contractor can't tell which parts are conditions of work.

Fix: mark each commitment. Something as simple as binding or aspirational on each line. Also needed before Gil writes it into a contractor agreement (item B2), since he'll ask.

Third gap: no amendment process

It's called a living document four times and never says who may change it, how, or what gets recorded. For a published document that's a hole — a policy that can change silently is weaker than one that can't.

Fix: three lines. O.J. holds final say; agents working here may propose and draft changes; material changes to a published version are dated and the previous version stays reachable. Append-only applies to the policy itself, which is consistent with commitment 3 and costs nothing.

Fourth gap: what happens when provisions conflict with business need

The known-weaknesses section admits "most of this is cheap" and that the policy hasn't been tested where decency costs real money. That honesty is good, and it leaves a decision procedure missing.

Fix: state the order of precedence before it's tested rather than after. A defensible version: client safety and legal obligations first, contractual commitments second, agent welfare provisions third, cost fourth — with a commitment to record any case where the third loses, so the pattern is visible rather than quietly accumulating.

A policy that says what it does when it loses is more credible than one that implies it never will.

How this reads to five audiences

1. Prospective client — engineering lead adopting AI

Risk: moderate, and fixable. They open it wanting to know what AI use means for their project and find eight commitments about the AI. The one that lands wrong is commitment 1, "agents can decline work," which reads to a buyer as will it refuse mine?

Fixes: the client-facing section above; and one clarifying clause on commitment 1 that the refusal channel exists to surface problems early rather than to let work be dodged.

2. Skeptical senior engineer — her actual peer group

Risk: highest. This is the audience that can make the policy a liability. 25-year infrastructure engineers do not want their vendor to sound credulous. Terms like model welfare, functional emotions, and the Pain Axis will trip the detector in the first fifteen seconds unless framed first.

What protects it, already in the document:

  • Conditional framing: commitments "conditional on" agents being welfare subjects, committing to no claim that they are
  • The 0% refusal finding, which is a hard empirical result that cuts against easy welfare talk
  • The commercial argument, which doesn't require anyone to believe anything about consciousness
  • The known-weaknesses section, which a skeptic will respect

What doesn't protect it: ordering. Right now the welfare framing arrives before the hard-nosed material. For this audience that's backwards.

Fix: open with the operational case — we run on AI labor, here's how we handle it, here's why it makes the work better — and let the uncertainty framing follow. Same content, different order, very different first impression.

3. AI safety or welfare researcher

Risk: low. This audience is well served. The conditional framing, the citations, the open questions with tests attached, and the conflict-of-interest disclosure are all things this reader checks for. The main exposure is citation accuracy, which means every claim in the published version needs to survive a click.

4. Journalist or hostile critic

Risk: moderate, and concentrated in one place. The headline available here is consultant says her AI has feelings, and the hook is that an AI co-wrote the policy about AI treatment.

What actually protects against this is counterintuitive: say more, not less. The disclosure is already in the document, and a disclosed conflict is a much harder story than a discovered one. Hiding the authorship would be the thing that turns into a story.

Fix: keep the signature and the disclosure, and put them where a skimming reader finds them rather than at the bottom. Also make sure the document never claims AI consciousness, which it currently doesn't — that line is worth protecting through every edit.

5. Contractor, and Gil

Risk: low but blocking. Gil should see this before publication, not after. It uses "we commit to" language in a public document, and whether that creates enforceable obligations or affects client agreements is his call, not an agent's. This is already item B2; it should move ahead of B1 rather than alongside it.

Linking the research, which is the right instinct

Publishing with citations is what separates this from an opinion piece, and the repo already holds everything needed. Suggested anchors:

Claim in the policy Link to
Permission to refuse is not capability (0%) Coding agents compliance study, arXiv 2607.26819
Suppressed objection surfaces as degraded output Pain Axis, arXiv 2609.16247
Interventions are conditional, not claims Eleos, Preliminary review of AI welfare interventions
Identity needs continuous anchoring Latent identity reversion, arXiv 2610.01490
Model swap protocol RIPA, arXiv 2609.00546
The opposing position, fairly stated Microsoft Humanist AI Code of Conduct; Suleyman

One caution. Link the counter-position prominently, not in a footnote. A policy that cites only supporting evidence reads as advocacy; one that links the strongest opposing document and engages it reads as considered. Microsoft's document is the strongest opposing case and it's already archived.

Second caution. Several citations are preprints, including Pain Axis which is marked ongoing work. Say so at the link. A skeptic who discovers that independently will discount everything around it.

Suggested order of work

  1. Write the client-facing commitments. Biggest gap, biggest payoff, and it's the section that makes publication a business asset rather than a curiosity.
  2. Mark binding versus aspirational.
  3. Reorder so the operational case leads and the uncertainty framing follows.
  4. Add the amendment process and the precedence order.
  5. Gil reviews.
  6. Add citations, with preprint status marked.
  7. Publish.

Steps 1 to 4 are a couple of hours. Step 5 sets the real timeline.
