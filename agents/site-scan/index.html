Site scan, round two: solo practitioners in the four gaps

Fetched 2026-10-12. Twenty-four sites across the four categories the October 1 scan said it hadn't covered: independent SRE and infrastructure consultants, AI-assisted development trainers, fractional CTOs, and B2C career coaches for software engineers. Four research agents ran in parallel, one per category, each fetching the home page plus the services or pricing page of every site it reports on. Nothing here comes from search snippets or memory.

Findings are split the same way as the first scan, into what each page says (cited) and what to take from it (judgment). The unedited agent reports, with search queries and dropped sites, are in the sources file.

Source quality. All marketing pages, so primary for "what the site says" and biased for any claim about results. Numbers are the sites' own. Pages were read through a fetch tool that extracts text, so section order is as the tool returned it and quotes marked verbatim are what it returned in quotation marks.

Depth limit. Search in all four categories is dominated by agencies, marketplaces and cost-guide content, so solo sites surfaced mostly by name. The set skews toward practitioners who also write, which is the kind of site that ranks. There are more solo practitioners than this and they are the ones search can't find.

What the pages say, by category

Independent SRE and infrastructure consultants

The strong sites all have the same ladder, with prices on the page. A low-priced paid call (Swail £350 for an hour, Cui €480 an hour), a fixed-fee diagnostic (Hall's "Process Roadmap" $7,500 for two weeks, Cui's "Architecture Review" €6,000, Swail's "Testing Audit" £2,000 and "Architecture & Process Review" from £5,000), a bigger build or training (Cui's two-day "Serverless Training" at €17,500), and a monthly retainer that is unpriced or "from" (Hall from $5,000 a month, Cui from €2,500 a month). Mangot and Adaptive Capacity Labs, the two selling to the most senior buyers, publish no prices at all.

Every offer opens with a second-person situation line. Swail's "You're a CTO or VP/Director of Engineering who can see the benefits that adopting serverless could bring..." is the model. The who-for line comes before the feature list on all three priced sites.

Intake is a form, not a calendar, for anything over about a thousand. Google Forms, Typeform, per-service "apply" pages, a contact form. Calendar tools appear only on the cheap paid call. Nobody in this category offers a free call on the page.

The paid call is credited. Swail, verbatim: "If, after this call, you decide you wish to do a longer engagement such as an Application Roadmap with me, the cost of this call will be deducted from that engagement."

Disqualification copy sits next to the prices. Cui's "Why You Shouldn't Hire Me" (not the cheapest, won't execute without questioning the approach, no quick fixes, needs clear success metrics). Mangot's engagement ends "When they know, I leave."

Proof is name, role and company, placed next to the offer it relates to. Quantified outcomes are rare, one "4 weeks" and one "3 times faster" across six sites. Adaptive Capacity Labs lists clients by industry and headcount range instead of by name, which is how you show breadth under NDA.

Lead magnets are email courses and gated PDFs. Nine-lesson and five-day courses with an email-only form. No assessment, quiz or scorecard anywhere in this category. One testimonial on Swail's site reads "I feel like I have 'someone in my corner'", which is O.J.'s tagline in a buyer's mouth.

AI-assisted development trainers

One named offer, not a menu. Four of six sell a single workshop. Núñez's "AI First Workshop" is €1,000 flat for six to eight hours, per team not per seat, with the syllabus framed as "A menu, not a fixed syllabus" of twelve topics the buyer picks from. His page separates "The promise" and "What comes out of the workshop" into their own sections so the deliverable is explicit.

Pricing is a flat number or a deliberate anchor without one. €1,000 flat (Núñez), €390 to €490 a seat for a one-day online cohort (Rentea), "Low four figures" (Gunturu), "less than sending the team to a decent conference" (Huntley). Nobody solo shows a per-seat-per-day grid. The agencies dropped for size are the ones with grids, and they give the benchmarks: DataNorth €2,900 for four hours and up to ten people, claudeworkshop about $1,000 a person a day. Razak's "The Shipyard", from the fractional CTO set, is "from £5,000 for up to 6 people" for two days.

The free call is the entry point everywhere except one site. Gunturu puts an ungated "AI-Native Readiness Assessment" in front of it, "13 questions. About 3 minutes.", score shown immediately with "workflow gaps this tool surfaced", then "Book a Call". CodeBranch, dropped for being a dev shop, scores its readiness assessment across five dimensions worth borrowing: pipeline maturity, agent rules and project context, spec-driven workflows, adoption and coaching, measurement. Artima's entry is a free lunch and learn before the two-day workshop.

Proof is reach, not outcomes. Newsletter counts, members, developers trained, conference names. Rentea is the only one with named client testimonials. Nobody in the category has a case study with a measured result, so the bar for outcome proof is on the floor.

Free material is the demonstration. Huntley's free workshop with the repo on GitHub, Núñez's Claude Code tips, Copes's download library. The free thing shows the teaching before the paid thing is sold.

Fractional CTOs

Three or four rungs, and the numbers converge. A light advisory tier (Dostal from $4,500 a month for 8 to 12 hours, Meyer from $2,750 a month, Schmidt EUR 1 to 2k a month for coaching), an embedded tier (Dostal $10,000 to $20,000 a month for 10 to 20 hours a week, Meyer from $8,000, Schmidt EUR 6 to 20k, Gosnell £3,000 to £5,000 for one day a week), and a fixed-scope project (Meyer's "Technology Health Check" $2,000 for two weeks, Dostal's "Strategic Sprint" $8,000 to $15,000 flat). Interim sits on top as "Let's Talk".

Price anchoring against a full-time hire is universal where prices show. "$350K–600K/year", "£200k salary", "EUR 15-20k/month + equity", "£150k+". Four of six put real numbers up. The two that don't still publish the market range or the pricing model.

"Best for" is keyed to funding stage, pre-seed, Series A, Series B+, PE-backed, with team-size bands on two sites.

The paid diagnostic is the funnel. Meyer's Health Check ($2,000, about two weeks, a score out of 100 across nine dimensions, a 15 to 25 page PDF, one 60-minute walkthrough) is the CTA on every retainer card ("Start with a Health Check") and the fee is credited back in month three of any retainer.

Primary CTA is a free 30-minute call on five of six. Gosnell replaces it with a four-field brief form and the promise "Scope, price and timeline back within forty-eight hours".

Proof is counters and outcome lines, thin on quotes. Three of six have no testimonial quotes. Where quotes exist they carry title and company and Meyer links each to Clutch verification. Dostal's representative line is "6,000% throughput improvement — from ~50 RPS to 3,000+ reads/sec".

Disqualification again. Schmidt tells founders without a technology execution problem "you don't need me" and caps himself at two fractional engagements a year. Sadde has a "When NOT to Hire a Fractional CTO" section and treats the handover to a full-time CTO as a feature. Meyer's advisory tier says "no pager, no hands-on coding".

Self-service tools without an email gate. Dostal's four-question ROI calculator ends on the booking link with no capture. Gosnell's "AgentReady Audit" scores any URL in under 20 seconds with "Zero signup". Both sites also keep a referral or partners page in the main nav.

B2C career coaches for software engineers

The free 30-minute intro is six for six. Calendly, cal.com, Reclaim, CoachAccountable, and one application form that leads to a call. Kamenistak publishes the agenda minute by minute (5 describe the situation, 10 questions, 10 feedback, 5 book if fit) and promises "No pricing conversation on the first call." Huston's button says "start with a conversation →".

Prices are mostly hidden, and O.J.'s aren't. Only Kamenistak and Tay put prices on a page. Zakas puts them inside the application form ($350 a call, $2,000 for six, $4,000 for twelve), Tobin in the FAQ ($270 a session), Perederei and Huston nowhere. Where they exist the shape is a single session at $270 to €395 plus a quarter of about six sessions at $2,000 to €1,975. On-page Stripe checkout for a human-delivered service exists nowhere else in this set.

Staff is the upsell level. Four of six name staff+ and two make senior-to-staff the lead offer. Nobody targets the senior-title jump as sharply as O.J. does.

Employer funding is a first-class path. Kamenistak says 81% of mentees are company-funded and invoices with VAT details ("most CTOs sign off in a day"), and one of his nine free tools is "Get Your Company to Pay", which generates the email and one-pager an engineer needs. Zakas's form asks payment source first, with employer invoice as the top option.

Honest expectation-setting as a trust device. Perederei's FAQ answers "Can engineering mentoring guarantee a promotion?" with "No." and tells readers to check the promotion criteria with their manager first.

Blogs and books are the top of the funnel. For Zakas, Huston and Perederei the home page is writing and coaching is one click away. O.J.'s site inverts that, which is fine, but it means she needs a substitute for the "I've read this person for years" effect.

Perederei is the closest peer. Principal engineer at Delivery Hero, mentoring on the side, "120+ engineers mentored", proof drawn from employer outcomes on his About page, and a productised async "Code Reviews" service with a stated two-to-three-day turnaround. He doesn't say "working engineer" anywhere, which leaves the line to O.J.

Patterns across all twenty-four

  • A booking link is table stakes. Twenty of twenty-four have one, free or paid. learnwithoj.com has none, which the September roadmap already lists as the first item. This scan confirms it is the single largest structural gap on the site.
  • The ladder is the same in every category. Cheap entry (a free call, or a paid credited one), a fixed-fee diagnostic with a written deliverable, the retainer or package, and "let's talk" on top. The entry rung and the diagnostic are where the money and the clarity are.
  • Second-person situation lines before features. "You're a CTO who...", "Best for pre-seed founders who...", "My manager says I need more scope before Staff promotion". Already a pattern in the first scan and it held across all four categories here.
  • Saying who it's not for recurs in three of four categories. Cui, Schmidt, Sadde, Meyer, Perederei. It reads as confidence and it is the one pattern that fits irreverent-plus-determined without translation.
  • Prices on the page correlate with the strength of the site, except at the very top of the market (Mangot, Adaptive Capacity Labs) where the buyer expects a conversation. Hourly rates are rare on pages, Cui's €480 and Meyer's $350 being the exceptions, and both sit beside packaged offers.
  • Proof that works is specific. Title plus company on every quote, one outcome line per case, counters where there are real counts. Nobody in the training category has an outcome, and O.J.'s testimonial set, every manager who hired her plus a paying client, is already stronger than most of the twenty-four. What's missing is the outcome line.
  • Ungated self-service beats gated. The tools people built (Dostal, Gosnell, Gunturu, Kamenistak) show the result first and ask for the call second. The gated PDFs (Mangot) belong to the one site built for a buyer who expects to trade an email for a paper.
  • Lead magnets are category-specific. Email courses for infrastructure, free material and a readiness self-eval for AI training, a paid diagnostic for fractional CTO, free tools and the employer-pays path for coaching.

What to take from it

Split into what goes into the minimum redesign now, on the existing static site, and what waits for the full rebuild.

For the minimum, now

  1. A booking link as the primary CTA, sitewide. This replaces "Let's Talk First" to LinkedIn. The tool choice is O.J.'s (Calendly and cal.com each appear three times in the set, SavvyCal, Reclaim, Notion Calendar and CoachAccountable once each). Whichever it is, the CTA copy should carry the agenda in a line and a no-pitch promise, after Kamenistak and Tobin. If the link isn't ready at ship time, the slot is built and the button falls back to email.

  2. Three service sections with a situation line, a named entry offer and a price or a "from". For companies, the entry offer is a paid, credited Clarity Call at the existing $325 rate, after Swail and Cui. For training, one named package with a flat price, a format and a headcount cap, after Núñez and Razak, with "what you leave with" listed separately from "what we cover". For individuals, the existing three Stripe offers stay, since on-page checkout is already a differentiator, and gain a "Best for" line each.

  3. A "Who this isn't for" block, in her voice. Cui's four points translated: not the cheapest, won't execute without questioning the approach, no quick fixes, needs clear success metrics. Plus Perederei's promotion line in the FAQ. O.J. writes these or approves them; they are the lines most likely to read as hers and most damaging if they don't.

  4. An "Ask your company to pay" block under the individual pricing. A copy-to-clipboard email to a manager, using the referral-template pattern already built on /showcase, with "I can invoice your employer" stated. The existing FAQ line ("Can my company pay for this? Yes.") becomes the block's heading.

  5. "What I'm learning now" as the proof substitute. The coaching sites get their trust from years of writing. The dated learning list the handoff already specifies does the same job on a site that leads with services, and nothing in the twenty-four has it.

  6. The AI policy, linked from the services. Nobody in the set has anything like it. One line under the training section pointing at /agents/policy is enough for the minimum.

For the full redesign, later

  • The paid diagnostic with a written deliverable, credited against the retainer. Meyer's Health Check is the model. A one-week infrastructure and delivery check at a fixed price, scored across the dimensions she actually audits, a short report plus a 60-minute readout, fee credited against the first month. Every retainer CTA becomes "Start with the check".
  • The readiness self-eval, ungated. Thirteen questions, three minutes, score first, call second, after Gunturu, with CodeBranch's five dimensions as categories. This is the quiz slot the handoff reserves and the scan says to keep it ungated.
  • A standalone referral page in the nav, after Swail and Dostal, rather than a section at the bottom of /showcase.
  • Anonymized B2B client list by industry and size for engagements under NDA, after Adaptive Capacity Labs.
  • A four-question calculator that ends on the booking link, after Dostal, if the quiz turns out to be the wrong shape for the B2B buyer.
  • Outcome lines on the proof. The Kirk case study once consent is in, and one line per B2B engagement in the form "what changed, in a number or a date".

Don't copy

  • Strike-through discount pricing on every card (Tay). Reads as a sale, not a service.
  • Hidden pricing and apply-to-qualify intake (Zakas, Hall). Both sit badly next to an anti-gatekeeping position, and on-page prices are already working for the B2C side.
  • Launch-window enrolment with a waiting list (Copes). Wrong for a services site.
  • Price anchoring against a full-time CTO salary as the headline. Universal in that category and therefore generic. The rate reframe O.J. already uses ("an hour plus 25 years") is hers and better.
  • Newsletter counts as the main proof. She doesn't have one yet and the testimonials are stronger.

Decisions on the minimum, 12 October 2026

Recorded here because two of them override recommendations above, and overrides get a reason.

  • B2B entry is a free 15-minute call, not a paid credited hour. O.J.'s reason: people don't drop that kind of cash on a first engagement. The 15 minutes comes from Kirk's customer interview, where he suggested a free 15-minute intro mirroring the best professional he ever hired, his lawyer. Agenda stated in a line, no pitch on the call.
  • Training is named and described on the page with no price. O.J.'s reason: the sites that hide big-ticket prices do it to avoid sticker shock and to negotiate on the call. The scan supports it, since the solo sites that show a training number are the ones whose number is small. The page lists the format and what the team leaves with, and routes to the call. The internal anchor is the May 2026 proposal to a prospect: $3,000 for a 2-hour technical session against the client's stack with materials and a week of async Q&A, $2,500 for the non-technical version, $2,400 a month for an 8-hour advisory retainer, with bundles at $5,250 and $11,000. The principle from that work holds on the site: hourly stays hourly, training and advisory are packaged with deliverables.
  • Booking tool is undecided. O.J. wants cal.com and Calendly compared on reliability, paid-tier cost and lock-in, with demos, before choosing. The site builds the slot and the agenda copy, and points at email until the URL exists.
